Stanford CS329A is the first graduate-level university course dedicated entirely to self-improving AI agents. Offered by Stanford University and taught by Aakanksha Chowdhery and Azalia Mirhoseini, the course covers the full pipeline of building agents that improve through test-time compute scaling, reinforcement learning, tool use, and open-ended evolution. It combines rigorous theory with hands-on research projects, making it the most comprehensive academic program on AI agents available in 2026.

Course Overview — What Is CS329A?#

CS329A, officially titled “Self-Improving Intelligent Agents,” is a graduate-level course launched by Stanford University in Fall 2025. It represents a significant milestone in AI education: the first time a top-tier university has offered a systematic curriculum on building AI agents that can improve their own performance over time.

The course spans 16 weeks and is organized into five core modules covering test-time compute scaling, learning from feedback, multi-step reasoning and planning, train-time scaling with reinforcement learning, and advanced topics including open-ended agent evolution, search-based agents, and memory augmentation. Each module builds on the previous one, creating a coherent narrative from foundational techniques to cutting-edge research.

What sets CS329A apart from other AI courses is its explicit focus on self-improvement — not just building agents that perform tasks, but agents that get better at those tasks through computation, feedback, and learning. This mirrors the trajectory of the broader AI field, where self-improving systems are increasingly seen as the path toward more capable and autonomous AI.

Who Should Take This Course? (Prerequisites & Target Audience)#

CS329A is designed for graduate students and advanced undergraduates with a strong foundation in machine learning and deep learning. The official prerequisites include familiarity with Python, PyTorch, and core ML concepts such as supervised learning, neural networks, and basic reinforcement learning.

The ideal candidate for this course is:

  • A graduate student in CS, AI, or related fields who wants to specialize in AI agents and autonomous systems
  • An industry practitioner with 2+ years of ML experience looking to transition into agent-based AI development
  • A researcher interested in the frontier of self-improving systems, RL for LLMs, and agentic frameworks
  • An advanced undergraduate (senior level) with strong ML coursework who wants exposure to cutting-edge AI research

The course assumes you can implement neural network architectures from scratch in PyTorch and have a working understanding of transformer architectures. If you have taken Stanford’s CS229 (Machine Learning) or CS231N (Deep Learning for Computer Vision), you will be well-prepared.

Instructors & Teaching Team#

The course is co-taught by two leading researchers in the field:

Aakanksha Chowdhery is a Staff Research Scientist at Google DeepMind and was a key contributor to the Gemini project. Her research focuses on large-scale machine learning systems, efficient training, and agent-based AI. She brings deep expertise in the engineering challenges of building production-grade AI systems.

Azalia Mirhoseini is a Research Scientist at Google DeepMind whose work spans reinforcement learning, chip design with RL (the pioneering circuit placement work published in Nature), and self-improving systems. Her research on using RL to optimize chip floorplanning demonstrated how self-improving agents can solve real-world engineering problems at scale.

Together, they bring complementary expertise: Chowdhery’s strength in large-scale ML systems and Mirhoseini’s pioneering work in RL-based optimization create a well-rounded instructional team.

The course also features guest lectures from prominent Google DeepMind researchers including Melvin Johnson, Denny Zhou, and Thang Luong, providing students with direct exposure to industry perspectives on agent deployment at scale.

Curriculum Deep Dive — 16 Lectures Across 5 Core Modules#

The CS329A curriculum is structured around five modules that progress from foundational techniques to advanced, open-ended topics. Here is a detailed breakdown of what each module covers.

Module 1: Test-Time Compute & Verification (Weeks 1-2)#

The course opens with one of the most active areas of current AI research: scaling computation at inference time. This module covers techniques that allow models to “think longer” before producing answers, including chain-of-thought reasoning, self-consistency, and tree-of-thought search.

Key topics include:

  • Test-time compute scaling laws and how they differ from training-time scaling
  • Verification techniques: process reward models vs. outcome reward models
  • Best-of-N sampling and rejection sampling for improving output quality
  • Self-consistency decoding and majority voting strategies

This module establishes the foundation for the entire course: the idea that an agent can improve its outputs by allocating more computation at inference time, without any weight updates.

Module 2: Learning from Feedback & Tool Use (Weeks 3-4)#

The second module shifts from static improvement to learning from interaction. Students learn how agents can incorporate feedback from humans, environments, and tools to improve their performance.

Key topics include:

  • Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and its variants
  • Tool use and function calling: how agents learn to use APIs, calculators, code interpreters, and search engines
  • Learning from execution feedback: using code execution results and environment rewards
  • Constitutional AI and self-critique mechanisms

A major focus of this module is the practical implementation of tool-use pipelines. Students work with agentic frameworks that allow LLMs to call external tools, observe results, and adjust their behavior accordingly.

Module 3: Multi-Step Reasoning & Planning (Weeks 5-6)#

Module 3 tackles one of the hardest problems in AI agent design: multi-step reasoning and long-horizon planning. Agents must not only answer questions but break down complex tasks into sequences of actions, execute them, and recover from failures.

Key topics include:

  • ReAct (Reasoning + Acting) framework for interleaved thought and action
  • Tree-of-Thoughts and Graph-of-Thoughts for structured reasoning
  • Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) for planning in agent environments
  • AB-MCTS (Alpha-Beta MCTS), a cutting-edge technique featured in the syllabus
  • Planning with learned world models

This module bridges the gap between simple prompt-based agents and systems capable of sustained, goal-directed behavior over many steps.

Module 4: Train-Time Scaling & RL (Weeks 7-8)#

The fourth module covers how agents can improve through training-time computation — actually updating model weights based on experience. This is where reinforcement learning takes center stage.

Key topics include:

  • Policy gradient methods and PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) for LLMs
  • Reward modeling and credit assignment over long trajectories
  • Scaling RL training: distributed RL architectures, experience replay, and efficiency
  • Online vs. offline RL for agent improvement
  • Self-play and iterative self-improvement loops

Students learn how to set up RL training pipelines for language agents, including the practical challenges of reward shaping, training stability, and compute management.

Module 5: Advanced Topics — Evolution, Search, Memory (Weeks 9-16)#

The second half of the course covers advanced and emerging topics in self-improving agents. This is where the curriculum becomes truly cutting-edge.

Key topics include:

  • Open-ended evolution: The AI Scientist, automated research pipelines, and agents that generate their own training data
  • Search and deep research agents: Building agents that can conduct multi-step research, synthesize information, and produce reports
  • Agentic frameworks for software engineering: SWE-agent, CodeAct, and autonomous coding agents
  • Memory augmentation: Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), episodic memory, and long-term memory for agents
  • Multi-agent systems: Coordination, communication, and competition between multiple agents
  • Robotics and embodied agents: Connecting self-improving agents to physical systems

This module draws heavily from the latest research, including papers published in 2024-2026. Students engage with primary research literature and implement state-of-the-art techniques.

Guest Lectures — Industry Insights from Google DeepMind#

One of the standout features of CS329A is its guest lecture series from Google DeepMind researchers. These sessions provide students with perspectives on how self-improving agent techniques are deployed at scale in production environments.

Melvin Johnson (Google DeepMind) covers large-scale language model deployment and the engineering challenges of serving agent-based systems. His lectures focus on the infrastructure required to support agents that interact with external tools and APIs in real-time.

Denny Zhou (Google DeepMind) presents on reasoning and chain-of-thought techniques, including his influential work on how LLMs can be prompted to reason step-by-step. His lectures connect the theoretical foundations of reasoning to practical implementation strategies.

Thang Luong (Google DeepMind) discusses machine translation, multilingual agents, and the intersection of language understanding with agentic systems. His perspective on building systems that operate across languages and cultures adds a global dimension to the course.

These guest lectures are not peripheral — they are integrated into the curriculum and students are expected to engage with the material through assignments and discussions.

Grading & Workload — What to Expect#

CS329A is a demanding course that requires significant time commitment. The grading breakdown reflects the course’s emphasis on hands-on implementation and research:

ComponentWeightDescription
Homework 1~17%Test-time compute and verification techniques
Homework 2~17%Tool use, feedback learning, and RLHF
Homework 3~17%Multi-step reasoning and planning
Project Proposal2.5%Research question, methodology, and timeline
Midterm Presentation10%Progress update and preliminary results
Final Project35%Full research project with report and code
Participation~1.5%Lecture engagement and peer feedback

The three homework assignments are designed to build practical skills incrementally. Each involves implementing techniques from scratch and running experiments. Students should expect to spend 10-15 hours per week on the course, with additional time during the final project phase.

The heavy weighting on the final project (35%) is intentional — the course is designed to produce research-ready outputs, not just theoretical understanding.

Research Project — The Core of the Course#

The final project is the centerpiece of CS329A. Students work in teams of 2-3 to design, implement, and evaluate a self-improving agent system. The project progresses through three milestones:

  1. Project Proposal (2.5%): A 2-3 page document outlining the research question, related work, proposed methodology, and evaluation plan. Students receive feedback from instructors and peers.

  2. Midterm Presentation (10%): A 10-minute presentation showing preliminary results, challenges encountered, and adjustments to the original plan. This serves as a checkpoint to ensure projects are on track.

  3. Final Report and Code (35%): A full research paper (6-8 pages) with accompanying code repository. Projects are evaluated on novelty, technical depth, experimental rigor, and clarity of presentation.

Example project topics from the course include:

  • Building a self-improving code generation agent that learns from test failures
  • Implementing an automated research agent that iteratively improves its search strategy
  • Developing a multi-agent system for collaborative problem-solving with self-play
  • Creating an RL-based agent that improves its tool-use strategy over time

The course provides API credits for LLM access, enabling students to run experiments at scale without personal cost.

Pros and Cons — An Honest Assessment#

Pros#

  1. First-of-its-kind curriculum: No other university course covers self-improving AI agents with this breadth and depth. CS329A fills a genuine gap in AI education.

  2. World-class instruction: Both Chowdhery and Mirhoseini are active researchers at Google DeepMind, bringing cutting-edge knowledge directly into the classroom.

  3. Industry integration: Guest lectures from DeepMind researchers provide real-world context that pure academic courses lack.

  4. Hands-on research: The 35% final project weighting means students produce genuine research outputs, not just homework solutions.

  5. Comprehensive scope: The course covers the full pipeline from test-time compute to open-ended evolution, giving students a complete mental model of the field.

  6. API credits provided: Students receive credits for LLM APIs, removing the financial barrier to running experiments.

Cons#

  1. High workload: 10-15 hours per week is significant, especially for students balancing other courses or jobs.

  2. Steep prerequisites: Students without a strong ML background will struggle. This is not an introductory course.

  3. Stanford tuition cost: As a Stanford course, it requires enrollment or auditing fees. The materials are not freely available online in a structured format.

  4. Rapidly evolving field: Some content may become dated quickly as the field of self-improving agents advances at a breakneck pace.

  5. Limited availability: The course is offered only at Stanford, limiting access for students at other institutions.

How to Enroll & Access Materials#

CS329A is offered through Stanford University’s Computer Science department. Enrollment options include:

  1. Stanford graduate students: Register through Axess during the enrollment period
  2. Stanford auditors: Available to Stanford affiliates with instructor permission
  3. Stanford Online: Some materials may be available through Stanford’s online platform
  4. YouTube playlist: Lecture recordings are available on the Stanford Online YouTube channel

For the most current information on enrollment, visit the official course page at cs329a.stanford.edu.

Frequently Asked Questions#

What prerequisites do I need for Stanford CS329A?#

You need a strong foundation in machine learning, deep learning, Python, and PyTorch. Familiarity with transformer architectures, supervised learning, and basic reinforcement learning is expected. Courses like Stanford CS229 or CS231N provide adequate preparation.

Is CS329A available online for remote students?#

Lecture recordings are available on the Stanford Online YouTube playlist, but the full course experience — including assignments, project feedback, and grading — requires Stanford enrollment. There is no fully remote version with credit at this time.

How does CS329A differ from other Stanford AI courses?#

CS329A is unique in its focus on self-improving agents. Unlike CS224N (NLP) or CS231N (Computer Vision), which focus on specific application domains, CS329A covers the meta-skill of building systems that improve themselves through computation, feedback, and learning.

What programming languages and frameworks are used in CS329A?#

The course uses Python and PyTorch as the primary programming environment. Students work with transformer-based language models, RL frameworks, and agentic tool-use libraries. API credits are provided for LLM access during the project phase.

Can I take CS329A without a machine learning background?#

No. The course assumes significant ML knowledge and moves quickly through advanced topics. Students without prior ML coursework will find the material inaccessible. It is recommended to complete at least one graduate-level ML course before enrolling.
