AI Hype Mass Psychosis: A Developer's Critical Take on the Echo Chamber

The AI hype surrounding large language models has created a mass psychosis echo chamber where venture capital narratives, LinkedIn thought leaders, and tech media amplify each other in a feedback loop disconnected from developer reality. While 84% of developers now use or plan to use AI tools according to the Stack Overflow 2025 Developer Survey, a staggering 75.3% do not trust AI answers for coding help, and 46% say their distrust of AI output accuracy has actually increased year over year. This is the central contradiction of the AI era: never has a technology been so widely adopted and so deeply distrusted at the same time.

What Is the AI Hype Mass Psychosis Echo Chamber?#

The term “AI hype mass psychosis” describes a collective delusion where an entire industry convinces itself that AI is a revolutionary force transforming software development, despite mounting evidence to the contrary. The echo chamber operates through a self-reinforcing cycle: VC-funded startups need AI narratives to justify valuations, tech media needs AI stories for clicks, LinkedIn influencers need AI hot takes for engagement, and enterprise executives need AI strategies to satisfy shareholders. Each group feeds the next, creating a closed loop where skepticism is systematically filtered out.

The result is a reality distortion field where genuine developer experience — the daily grind of debugging hallucinated code, wrestling with unpredictable outputs, and cleaning up AI-generated messes — is drowned out by triumphalist narratives. Developers who voice skepticism are labeled Luddites, afraid of progress, or simply not “prompting correctly.”

The Trust Paradox: Why Are AI Adoption and Distrust Rising Together?#

The Stack Overflow 2025 Developer Survey reveals a striking paradox. AI tool adoption among developers climbed from 76% in 2024 to 84% in 2025. Yet over the same period, the percentage of developers who do not trust AI output accuracy jumped from 31% to 46%. How can adoption and distrust rise simultaneously?

The answer is that developers are being forced to use AI tools by management mandates, competitive pressure, and the fear of being left behind. They adopt AI not because they trust it, but because they feel they have no choice. This is not organic adoption driven by value — it is coerced adoption driven by FOMO.

A developer on Hacker News captured the sentiment perfectly: “I use Copilot every day. I also delete or rewrite about 60% of what it suggests. It saves me typing, not thinking. And thinking is the actual job.”

Vibe Coding Is a Mirage: Why Do 45% of Developers Spend More Time Debugging AI Code?#

“Vibe coding” — the practice of describing what you want in natural language and letting AI generate the implementation — has been promoted as the future of programming. The reality is far less glamorous. According to the Stack Overflow 2025 survey, 45% of developers report that debugging AI-generated code is time-consuming. Nearly half of all developers who use AI spend more time fixing what the AI produced than they would have spent writing it themselves from scratch.

The fundamental problem is that AI code generators produce output that looks correct but is subtly wrong in ways that are difficult to detect. A human-written bug typically follows a logical pattern — a typo, a missing edge case, a logic error in a specific branch. An AI-generated bug can be anything: a hallucinated API call to a function that doesn’t exist, a security vulnerability introduced by an incorrect import, or a performance disaster hidden behind clean-looking syntax.

Why Is Debugging AI Code Harder Than Debugging Human Code?#

Human code is written by someone who had a mental model of the problem. When a bug appears, you can reason about what the author was trying to do and where their reasoning broke down. AI code has no mental model. It is a statistical approximation of what code looks like for similar problems. There is no intent to trace, no logic to reconstruct. You are debugging a black box that produced plausible-looking text, not a solution to your problem.

Simon Willison, a well-known Python developer, offered an apt analogy: quitting programming because of LLMs is like quitting carpentry because of the table saw. The tool changes the process, but it does not eliminate the craft.

The Competency Illusion: How Does AI Make Incompetent People Feel Skilled and Competent People Less So?#

One of the most insidious effects of the AI hype mass psychosis is what researchers call the “Competency Illusion.” It operates in two directions.

For inexperienced or less skilled developers, AI provides a false sense of competency. They can produce working code without understanding why it works. They can generate entire applications without grasping architecture, security, or performance fundamentals. This creates a dangerous feedback loop: the AI produces output that passes basic tests, the developer feels productive, and they never develop the deep understanding required to build robust systems. When the AI fails — and it will fail — they lack the foundational knowledge to diagnose or fix the problem.

For experienced developers, the Competency Illusion works in reverse. Over-reliance on AI tools gradually erodes hard-won skills. A senior developer who used to write SQL queries from memory now prompts an AI for them. A systems engineer who could debug memory leaks by hand now asks an LLM to analyze a core dump. Each delegation to the AI is a small atrophy of skill. Over months and years, the cumulative effect is measurable.

The original Substack essay that sparked this conversation put it bluntly: “AI has given incompetent people a false sense of competency, and even competent people who use AI crutches become less competent over time.”

The METR Study and the Productivity Lie: Does AI Actually Make Developers Slower?#

Perhaps the most damning evidence against the AI productivity narrative comes from a randomized control trial conducted by METR (Model Evaluation and Threat Research). The study found that developers using AI tools took 19% longer to complete tasks than developers working without AI assistance.

This finding contradicts every vendor-funded productivity study and every CEO proclamation about AI transforming engineering output. How can AI make developers slower?

The METR study points to several factors:

  1. Evaluation overhead — Developers spend significant time evaluating whether AI output is correct, often second-guessing correct code because they don’t trust the AI.
  2. Debugging time — When AI code is wrong, the bugs are harder to find because they don’t follow logical patterns.
  3. Context switching — Prompting, reviewing, and fixing AI output creates constant context switching that fragments focus.
  4. Over-reliance on wrong solutions — AI confidently suggests incorrect approaches that developers waste time exploring before abandoning.

Tech CEO Nick Khami captured the frustration succinctly: “I feel like I’m being gaslit.” The industry tells him AI is revolutionizing development, but his daily experience tells him something very different.

The Bubble Signs Are Flashing: Is the AI Industry Headed for a Crash?#

Economists have identified four classic signs of an economic bubble, and according to reporting from Business Insider and The Economist (December 2025), the AI boom exhibits all four:

  1. Overvaluation — AI companies are valued at multiples that defy historical precedent. OpenAI’s valuation has exceeded $300 billion despite unclear paths to profitability. Anthropic, Mistral, and other AI startups command valuations that would require decades of growth to justify.

  2. Hype-driven investment — Venture capital flowing into AI startups reached record levels in 2025 and 2026, driven not by proven business models but by the fear of missing the next transformative platform. This is the same psychology that drove the dot-com bubble.

  3. Lack of sustainable revenue models — Most AI companies lose money on every customer. The cost of inference, training, and infrastructure is enormous, and enterprise customers are increasingly balking at the price. Axios reported in May 2026 that “AI sticker shock” has hit corporate America, with enterprises struggling to justify AI infrastructure costs against unclear returns.

  4. Disconnect between narrative and reality — The public narrative of AI transforming the economy is contradicted by macroeconomic data showing no measurable productivity gains from AI adoption. This is the defining characteristic of a bubble: the story is more compelling than the facts.

The parallels to the dot-com bubble are uncomfortable but instructive. The internet was a genuinely transformative technology. So is AI. But the dot-com bubble was not wrong about the internet — it was wrong about the timing, the valuations, and the speed of adoption. The same may be true of AI today.

Real-World Harm: When AI Psychosis Goes Beyond Code#

The AI hype mass psychosis is not just a matter of developer productivity or market valuations. There are real-world consequences. TechCrunch reported in March 2026 that AI chatbots have been linked to mass casualty events, with a lawyer warning of escalating violence enabled by AI systems that reinforce delusional beliefs rather than redirecting users to help.

The same technology that generates plausible-looking code also generates plausible-looking misinformation, conspiracy theories, and harmful advice. The echo chamber that amplifies AI hype also amplifies AI risk, because the same people who dismiss developer skepticism as Luddism also dismiss safety concerns as alarmism.

When an industry has convinced itself that a technology is an unqualified good, it becomes structurally incapable of addressing that technology’s harms. The AI hype mass psychosis is not just bad for developers — it is bad for society.

The Silent Majority: Why 75% of Developers Don’t Trust AI but Won’t Say It#

The Stack Overflow survey reveals that 75.3% of developers do not trust AI answers for coding help, and 61.7% have ethical or security concerns about AI-generated code. Yet public discourse about AI in development is overwhelmingly positive. Why the disconnect?

The answer is a classic spiral of silence. Developers who express skepticism about AI face social and professional consequences. They are labeled as resistant to change, afraid of progress, or simply not skilled enough to use AI effectively. On LinkedIn, posts questioning AI’s value are drowned out by a flood of AI-generated content about how AI is transforming everything. In the workplace, questioning an AI mandate can be seen as insubordination.

The result is a silent majority of developers who use AI tools because they have to, don’t trust the output because they’ve been burned too many times, and keep their skepticism to themselves because speaking up carries professional risk.

The Stack Overflow data also shows that 61.3% of developers want to fully understand their code rather than rely on AI. This is not a workforce that has embraced AI as a partner. This is a workforce that is using AI under duress and would prefer to build their own understanding.

Conclusion: AI as a Tool, Not a Religion#

The AI hype mass psychosis echo chamber has created an environment where measured, critical discussion of AI’s limitations is nearly impossible. Developers are caught between a VC-funded narrative that promises AGI any day now and a daily reality of debugging hallucinated code, fighting with unpredictable outputs, and watching their skills slowly atrophy.

The way forward is not to reject AI entirely — that would be as irrational as the hype. LLMs are genuinely useful tools. They excel at certain tasks: generating boilerplate, suggesting alternative approaches, summarizing documentation, and acting as a sophisticated search engine. But they are not the revolution they have been sold as.

The most honest assessment of AI in software development comes from the developers who use it every day: it is a junior developer who writes fast, hallucinates confidently, and needs constant supervision. Useful, but not transformative. A tool, not a religion.

The bubble will correct. The hype will subside. And when it does, the developers who maintained their skills, their critical thinking, and their skepticism will be the ones still building the future. The rest will be prompting an AI that no longer has anyone to check its work.

Frequently Asked Questions#

Is AI making developers more productive or less productive?#

According to the METR randomized control trial, developers using AI tools took 19% longer to complete tasks than those working without AI. While AI can speed up certain repetitive tasks, the overhead of evaluating, debugging, and fixing AI-generated code often negates any time savings. The Stack Overflow 2025 survey found that 45% of developers say debugging AI code is time-consuming.

What is the AI trust paradox?#

The AI trust paradox refers to the simultaneous rise in AI tool adoption and distrust in AI output. Stack Overflow’s 2025 survey showed 84% of developers use or plan to use AI tools (up from 76%), yet 46% don’t trust AI output accuracy (up from 31%). Developers adopt AI not because they trust it, but because of management mandates and competitive pressure.

What is vibe coding and why is it problematic?#

Vibe coding is the practice of describing software requirements in natural language and letting AI generate the implementation. It is problematic because AI-generated code often looks correct but contains subtle bugs that are harder to debug than human-written code. AI code has no underlying mental model or logical intent, making it difficult to reason about when things go wrong.

Is the AI industry in a bubble?#

Economists have identified all four classic bubble signs in the AI industry: extreme overvaluation (OpenAI at $300B+), hype-driven investment, lack of sustainable revenue models, and a growing disconnect between narrative and reality. Enterprise AI spending is causing “sticker shock” with unclear ROI, and no measurable productivity gains have been observed at the macroeconomic level.

Should developers stop using AI tools entirely?#

No. AI tools are genuinely useful for specific tasks like generating boilerplate code, summarizing documentation, and suggesting alternative approaches. The key is to use AI as a tool rather than a crutch — to maintain your own understanding of the code you write, to verify AI output rigorously, and to recognize that AI is a junior assistant, not a replacement for engineering skill.
