Commodification of Intelligence: Circular AI Deals and What They Mean for Costs

Circular AI deals — where AI companies invest in each other and commit to spending on each other's services — are reshaping how intelligence is priced and funded.

Circular AI deals — where the same small group of hyperscalers, AI labs, and chipmakers invest in each other and simultaneously commit to spending billions on each other’s services — are transforming artificial intelligence from a speculative technology into a funded infrastructure asset class. These arrangements, which now total well over a trillion dollars in committed capital, are accelerating the commodification of intelligence itself, turning GPUs and data center capacity into fungible resources much like electricity, oil, or natural gas.

What Are Circular AI Deals? — A Simple Explanation#

A circular AI deal occurs when Company A invests in Company B, and Company B simultaneously commits to spending a large portion of that investment on Company A’s products or services. The money flows in a loop rather than entering the broader economy. For example, Microsoft invests $13 billion in OpenAI, and OpenAI commits to spending $250 billion on Microsoft’s Azure cloud platform. The cash goes from Microsoft to OpenAI and then right back to Microsoft as cloud revenue.

This structure is not unique to AI. Japanese commodities trading houses (sogo shosha) have used similar circular financing arrangements since the 1960s to fund oil refineries, copper mines, and LNG terminals. The investor provides capital, and the recipient agrees to buy the investor’s infrastructure services — creating a guaranteed revenue stream that justifies the original investment.

What makes today’s AI circular deals different is their sheer scale. The amounts involved — hundreds of billions of dollars — are unprecedented outside of sovereign debt markets. And unlike traditional commodity infrastructure, the “product” being funded is intelligence itself: the compute power required to train and run the world’s most advanced AI models.

The total committed capital in circular AI arrangements now exceeds $1.5 trillion when including the Nvidia Ohio backstop and Oracle’s Stargate project. To put that in perspective, that is larger than the GDP of all but 12 countries in the world.

Why Circular Deals Are Not Inherently Bad — The Commodity Infrastructure Analogy#

It is tempting to label circular AI deals as fraudulent or as signs of a bubble. But the commodity infrastructure analogy suggests a more nuanced interpretation.

When a Japanese trading company finances a copper mine in Chile, it does so with a guaranteed offtake agreement: the mine’s output is pre-sold to the trading company’s industrial customers. The financing is secured against future production. The deal is circular in the sense that the financier is also the end customer, but it is rational because the underlying demand for copper is real.

The same logic applies to AI. Microsoft needs Azure to be the dominant cloud platform for AI workloads. OpenAI needs massive compute capacity to train increasingly capable models. A circular deal aligns both interests: Microsoft gets guaranteed Azure revenue, and OpenAI gets the compute it needs without having to raise all the capital upfront.

“These structures have been used in commodity markets for over 60 years,” notes Emerging Trajectories in their analysis. “GPUs and data centers are becoming fungible commodities, making circular deals rational and necessary.”

The key distinction is that commodity circular deals are backed by real end-user demand — people actually need copper, oil, and electricity. The question for AI is whether the end-user demand for intelligence services will grow fast enough to justify the committed infrastructure spending.

The Commodification of Intelligence: GPUs as Fungible Commodities#

The circular deal phenomenon is both a cause and a consequence of the commodification of intelligence. As GPUs become standardized, interchangeable computing resources, and as data center capacity becomes a tradable asset, intelligence itself is being transformed from a bespoke service into a fungible utility.

Consider the trajectory of GPU pricing. In 2022, Nvidia H100 GPUs were scarce, allocation-controlled, and priced at a significant premium. By mid-2026, multiple GPU generations are available from Nvidia, AMD, and custom ASIC providers. Cloud GPU spot pricing has emerged, and secondary markets for compute credits are developing. This is the classic pattern of commodification: a scarce, differentiated product becomes a standardized, price-competitive commodity.

The implications for AI costs are profound:

  1. Compute costs are falling. As GPU supply expands and competition increases, the per-token cost of inference is dropping rapidly. What cost $10 per million tokens in 2024 may cost $0.10 by 2027.

  2. Intelligence becomes a utility. Just as businesses do not build their own power plants, they will increasingly not build their own AI infrastructure. They will buy intelligence by the token from hyperscaler cloud platforms.

  3. Circular deals accelerate commodification. By guaranteeing massive demand for compute, circular deals incentivize hyperscalers to build capacity at scale, which in turn drives down unit costs for everyone.

  4. The margin shifts from compute to data and distribution. When compute is a commodity, the competitive advantage shifts to who has the best data, the best distribution, and the best application-layer products.

The Real Risk: Valuation Inflation and the Untested Stress Test#

While circular AI deals are structurally rational, they carry a specific and serious risk: valuation inflation based on committed spending from within a small circle, rather than genuine end-user demand.

Here is how the risk works. OpenAI reports $5 billion in annual revenue but $8.5 billion in annualized losses. Its valuation of $852 billion (from the March 2026 funding round) implies a price-to-revenue multiple of over 170x. A significant portion of OpenAI’s revenue comes from Microsoft Azure credits — which themselves originated from Microsoft’s investment in OpenAI. The circularity means that revenue and spending are partially endogenous to the system.

“If you strip out the circular revenue, what is the real end-user demand for OpenAI’s products?” asks Compute Forecast. “That is the question the market has not yet answered.”

The $785 billion in annual hyperscaler capex has never faced a demand moderation stress test. Since the AI boom began in late 2022, demand for AI compute has grown exponentially every quarter. But exponential growth cannot continue indefinitely. At some point, growth will moderate — and when it does, the circular deals that looked rational during the boom may reveal overcapacity and inflated commitments.

Compute Forecast identifies the core risk: “The risk is not collapse but inflated valuations based on committed spending from within a small circle.” In other words, the system is not necessarily fragile, but it may be significantly overvalued relative to genuine market demand.

The $250 Billion Question: Nvidia’s Ohio Backstop and What It Signals#

The most dramatic circular deal to date is Nvidia’s reported discussion of a $250 billion backstop for OpenAI’s planned 10-gigawatt AI data center campus in Ohio, as reported by CNBC and the Wall Street Journal in July 2026.

To understand the scale: 10 gigawatts is enough to power approximately 8 million homes. It is more than the entire electricity consumption of some small countries. The data center campus would be the largest single electricity consumer in the United States.

Nvidia’s role as a backstop guarantor is significant because it represents a new level of entanglement. Nvidia is not just selling GPUs to OpenAI — it is guaranteeing the financing for the infrastructure that will house those GPUs. If OpenAI cannot pay its data center bills, Nvidia is on the hook for $250 billion.

This deal signals several things:

  • Nvidia’s confidence in long-term AI demand is extremely high. A $250 billion guarantee is not a casual bet.
  • The lines between chipmaker, cloud provider, and AI lab are completely blurred. Nvidia is now effectively a financier of AI infrastructure, not just a hardware supplier.
  • The scale of individual deals is growing faster than the underlying market. A $250 billion backstop for a single data center campus is unprecedented in any industry.

The Los Angeles Times reported on July 29, 2026, that Nvidia is working on fresh AI deals worth more than $750 billion in total. If even a fraction of these deals materialize, the circular AI deal market will exceed $2 trillion by the end of 2026.

Dot-Com Echoes vs. Genuine Demand — Two Views#

The circular AI deal phenomenon has drawn comparisons to the dot-com bubble of 1999-2000. Jim Cramer, who lived through the dot-com era as a hedge fund manager, warned on CNBC on July 27, 2026: “I lived through 2000. I don’t want the sequel.”

Cramer’s concern centers on the circular financing structure. During the dot-com bubble, companies invested in each other, created reciprocal revenue arrangements, and inflated each other’s valuations. When the music stopped, the circularity collapsed, and so did the valuations. Nvidia’s shares fell more than 4% on the news of the Ohio backstop, suggesting that some investors share Cramer’s concern.

However, there are important differences between the dot-com era and today’s AI landscape:

Industry insiders argue that the underlying AI demand is real and growing. Enterprise AI adoption, AI-assisted coding, and AI-powered research are generating genuine revenue. The question is not whether AI has value — it clearly does — but whether the valuations and committed spending reflect that value or are inflated by circular financing.

What Happens If AI Demand Growth Moderates?#

The most important scenario to consider is what happens when AI compute demand growth inevitably moderates from its current exponential trajectory.

In a demand moderation scenario:

  1. Hyperscalers face overcapacity. The $785 billion in annual capex was built for exponential growth. If growth drops to 30-40% annually, a significant portion of that capacity becomes surplus.

  2. Circular commitments become liabilities. OpenAI’s $250 billion Azure commitment, Anthropic’s $100 billion AWS commitment — these were negotiated when growth seemed infinite. If revenue growth slows, these commitments become crushing obligations.

  3. Backstop guarantors are tested. Nvidia’s $250 billion Ohio backstop would be called if OpenAI cannot pay. Even for a company with Nvidia’s market cap, a $250 billion loss would be devastating.

  4. The AI debt market amplifies losses. Emerging Trajectories warns of a potential multi-trillion-dollar AI debt market with mortgage-backed-security-like systemic risk emerging by 2029. If circular deals move off balance sheets into securitized debt products, a demand moderation could trigger a cascading financial crisis.

  5. Consolidation accelerates. Weaker AI labs and smaller cloud providers would be acquired or shut down. The circular deal structure favors incumbents who can afford to play the long game.

The most likely outcome is not a catastrophic collapse but a period of painful adjustment. Valuations would correct, some deals would be renegotiated, and the pace of infrastructure buildout would slow. The companies with genuine end-user demand and strong balance sheets — Microsoft, Amazon, Google — would emerge stronger. The question is which AI labs and chipmakers would survive the adjustment.

The Bottom Line for AI Costs and the Future of Intelligence Pricing#

The commodification of intelligence, accelerated by circular AI deals, is driving a structural decline in the cost of AI compute. This is good news for businesses and consumers who use AI services. Lower compute costs mean cheaper API calls, more capable free-tier models, and broader AI adoption across the economy.

However, the circular deal structure introduces a layer of financial engineering that obscures the true cost of intelligence. When Microsoft books OpenAI’s Azure spending as revenue, and OpenAI counts Microsoft’s investment as capital, the system’s economics are not transparent. The real cost of intelligence — what end users actually pay — may be higher than it appears because the infrastructure costs are being subsidized by circular capital flows.

For businesses evaluating AI investments, the key takeaway is that AI costs will continue to fall in the medium term as compute commoditizes, but the financial system supporting AI infrastructure carries risks that could lead to price volatility in a downturn. The smart strategy is to build AI capabilities now, but avoid long-term lock-in contracts that assume today’s pricing and availability will persist indefinitely.

Intelligence is becoming a commodity. The question is not whether it will be cheap — it will be — but whether the financial infrastructure built to fund its creation can withstand the transition from exponential growth to sustainable maturity.

Frequently Asked Questions#

What exactly is a circular AI deal? A circular AI deal is an arrangement where one company invests in another, and the recipient commits to spending a significant portion of that investment on the investor’s products or services. For example, Microsoft invested $13 billion in OpenAI, and OpenAI committed to spending $250 billion on Microsoft’s Azure cloud platform, creating a circular flow of capital.

Are circular AI deals illegal or fraudulent? No, circular AI deals are not inherently illegal or fraudulent. Similar structures have been used in commodity infrastructure financing for decades, particularly by Japanese trading companies since the 1960s. However, they can create skewed incentives and obscure the true level of genuine end-user demand, which concerns some analysts and regulators.

How much money is involved in circular AI deals? The total committed capital in circular AI arrangements exceeds $1.5 trillion as of mid-2026. Major deals include Microsoft’s $13 billion investment in OpenAI paired with OpenAI’s $250 billion Azure commitment, Amazon’s $5 billion investment in Anthropic with Anthropic’s $100 billion AWS commitment, and Nvidia’s reported $250 billion backstop for OpenAI’s Ohio data center campus.

Could circular AI deals cause a financial crisis? Analysts at Emerging Trajectories warn that a potential multi-trillion-dollar AI debt market with mortgage-backed-security-like systemic risk could emerge by 2029. If circular deals move off balance sheets into securitized debt products and AI demand growth moderates, the resulting losses could cascade through the financial system. However, most current deals are on the balance sheets of well-capitalized companies like Microsoft, Amazon, and Nvidia, which limits systemic risk in the near term.

What does commodification of intelligence mean for AI pricing? Commodification means that AI compute — GPUs, data center capacity, and inference tokens — is becoming a standardized, fungible resource with falling prices. As GPU supply expands and competition increases, the per-token cost of AI inference is dropping rapidly. This makes AI more accessible to businesses and consumers but shifts competitive advantage from compute ownership to data, distribution, and application-layer innovation.
