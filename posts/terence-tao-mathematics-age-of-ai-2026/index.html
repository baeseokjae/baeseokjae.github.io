Introduction — Terence Tao at ICM 2026: A Watershed Moment for Mathematics#

At the 2026 International Congress of Mathematicians (ICM) in Philadelphia, Fields Medalist Terence Tao delivered a landmark public lecture titled “Mathematics in the Age of AI.” His message was clear: artificial intelligence is not replacing mathematicians — but it is fundamentally changing what it means to do mathematics. Tao argued that the combination of AI tools and formal verification systems like the Lean proof assistant is enabling a new kind of mathematical research at unprecedented scale, while also warning that mathematics’ conservative culture and high barriers to collaboration must evolve to fully harness these capabilities.

Why Has Mathematics Remained So Resistant to Collaboration?#

Mathematics has long been the outlier among scientific disciplines when it comes to collaboration. While fields like biology, physics, and medicine have seen dramatic increases in team sizes and co-authorship over the past century, mathematics has remained stubbornly individualistic. According to Tao’s research, the average number of authors per mathematics paper has grown from approximately 1.5 to only 2.5 over several decades — a modest increase compared to the explosion of multi-author papers in other sciences.

Tao identified three structural barriers that have historically prevented mathematics from scaling collaboration:

1. The Verification Problem. In most sciences, experimental results can be independently replicated. A biologist can rerun an experiment; a physicist can rebuild an apparatus. But in mathematics, verifying a proof requires a human to understand and check every logical step — a process that can take months or years for complex results. Without a way to trust that a collaborator’s work is correct without fully re-deriving it, large-scale collaboration becomes impractical.

2. The Specialization Gap. Modern mathematics is deeply fragmented into subfields. A topologist and an algebraic geometer may struggle to understand each other’s work, let alone collaborate productively. This linguistic and conceptual barrier means that even when mathematicians want to collaborate, the overhead of bridging subfield gaps is often prohibitive.

3. The Incentive Problem. Academic mathematics rewards individual brilliance. The field’s most prestigious prizes — the Fields Medal, the Abel Prize, the Wolf Prize — are awarded to individuals or small groups. The culture prizes deep, elegant proofs over broad, collaborative projects. As Tao noted, a mathematician who spends years on a solo proof of a major theorem is celebrated; a team that systematically solves thousands of medium-difficulty problems may receive less recognition.

These barriers have kept mathematics as what Tao calls a “cottage industry” — brilliant individuals working in relative isolation — long after other sciences industrialized their research processes.

How Does Formal Verification Enable Trustless Mathematical Collaboration?#

Tao’s central thesis at ICM 2026 was that formal verification — not AI alone — is the truly transformative technology for mathematics. The Lean proof assistant, an interactive theorem prover developed by Microsoft Research and an open-source community, allows mathematicians to write proofs in a language that a computer can check automatically. When a proof is formalized in Lean, any mathematician anywhere in the world can verify its correctness in seconds, without needing to understand the original reasoning.

This is what Tao calls “trustless collaboration” — a term borrowed from blockchain technology. In a trustless system, participants do not need to trust each other’s expertise or honesty because the system itself guarantees correctness. For mathematics, this is revolutionary.

The Lean community has already formalized major results including the Kepler conjecture (through the Flyspeck project), the Liquid Tensor Experiment, and most recently, the Equational Theories Project. Each of these demonstrates that formal verification can handle proofs of real mathematical significance, not just toy problems.

What Was the Equational Theories Project and Why Does It Matter?#

The Equational Theories Project stands as the most dramatic demonstration yet of what formal verification plus AI can achieve in mathematics. Launched on GitHub in September 2024, the project brought together approximately 50 collaborators — including professional mathematicians, computer scientists, and skilled amateurs — to systematically explore the landscape of equational laws governing magmas (algebraic structures with a single binary operation).

The project’s goal was deceptively simple: determine all implication relationships between 4,694 equational laws. This meant solving 22,028,942 implication problems — a task that would be utterly impossible for any individual mathematician or even a traditional research team.

The results were staggering. In just three months, the team solved all 22 million problems. The final paper was published on arXiv in December 2025. This represents a paradigm shift from what Tao calls “case study” mathematics — investigating one problem at a time in depth — to “large-scale survey” mathematics, where thousands or millions of related problems are solved systematically.

The project succeeded because of a powerful synergy:

  • Formal verification (Lean) provided the trustless foundation. Any contributor could submit a proof, and the Lean checker would verify it automatically. No one needed to manually review every submission.
  • AI tools handled the “long tail” of medium-difficulty problems — the thousands of routine implications that would be tedious for humans but are straightforward for automated reasoning systems.
  • Human insight focused on the genuinely hard problems — the small fraction of implications that required creative mathematical thinking.

This division of labor — humans handling creative insight, AI handling routine work, and formal verification ensuring correctness — is the model Tao believes will define 21st-century mathematics.

How Is AI Augmenting Mathematical Research Today?#

Tao is careful to distinguish between AI that generates plausible-sounding but unverified output and AI that genuinely advances mathematical knowledge. He warns against what he calls “slop” — AI-generated mathematics that looks convincing but contains subtle errors. The key, he argues, is the “Generate → Verify → Feedback → Revise” loop.

The Generate-Verify Loop#

In this model, AI systems generate candidate proofs or conjectures, formal verification tools check them for correctness, the results are fed back to refine the AI’s approach, and the cycle repeats. This loop ensures that AI output is not just plausible but mathematically rigorous.

AlphaEvolve: AI Breaking Human Records#

One of the most concrete examples Tao cited was AlphaEvolve, a DeepMind collaboration that broke human records in hexagon packing optimization problems. These are problems where the goal is to arrange shapes in the most efficient possible configuration — a class of problems with applications ranging from materials science to logistics. AlphaEvolve discovered configurations that no human mathematician had found, demonstrating that AI can genuinely extend the frontier of mathematical knowledge, not just automate existing techniques.

Can AI Really Bring Division of Labor to Mathematics?#

At an OpenAI event in March 2026, Tao proposed a provocative idea: AI could, for the first time in history, introduce a genuine division of labor to mathematical research. In every other scientific and industrial field, specialization and role differentiation have driven productivity gains. Mathematics has remained an outlier — a field where the same person is expected to conceive the problem, develop the proof, write it up, check it, and communicate it.

Tao envisions a future where mathematical research involves distinct specialized roles:

  • Problem Conceiver: Identifies important open problems and formulates research questions
  • Proof Writer: Develops the high-level logical structure of proofs
  • Formalizer: Translates informal proofs into machine-checkable formal language (Lean, Coq, etc.)
  • AI Operator: Designs and runs AI systems for conjecture generation, automated proving, and pattern discovery
  • Verification Specialist: Ensures that AI-generated mathematics meets rigorous standards
  • Communicator: Translates technical results for broader mathematical and public audiences

This division of labor would dramatically lower the barrier to entry for mathematical research. A talented programmer with strong logical skills but no formal mathematics training could contribute as a formalizer. A domain expert who struggles with formalization could focus on problem conception. The result would be a much larger, more diverse mathematical workforce.

What Does the Future Hold for Mathematics in the Age of AI?#

Tao’s vision for the future of mathematics is neither utopian nor dystopian. He does not believe AI will replace mathematicians, nor does he think everything will remain the same. Instead, he sees a transformation analogous to what happened when computers entered other fields.

Key Takeaways from Tao’s ICM 2026 Lecture#

1. Formal verification is the foundation. AI-generated mathematics is only as trustworthy as its verification system. The Lean proof assistant and similar tools are not optional extras — they are the essential infrastructure that makes large-scale AI-assisted mathematics possible.

2. Scale changes the nature of the work. The Equational Theories Project demonstrated that solving 22 million problems is qualitatively different from solving one. At scale, the bottlenecks shift from individual insight to system design, data management, and collaboration protocols.

3. Mathematics will not speed up — but it will broaden. Tao made a counterintuitive point: AI will generate more proofs, but mathematics as a discipline will not necessarily accelerate. The rate at which the field can absorb, understand, and build upon new results is limited by human cognition. Instead, AI will enable mathematics to broaden — exploring vast landscapes of problems that were previously inaccessible.

4. Citizen mathematics is coming. Just as citizen science projects like Galaxy Zoo and eBird have allowed non-scientists to contribute to astronomy and biology, “citizen mathematics” could allow non-mathematicians to contribute to mathematical research. The Equational Theories Project already included skilled amateurs alongside professionals. As formal verification lowers the trust barrier, this trend will accelerate.

5. The human element remains essential. Tao emphasized that mathematics is ultimately a human endeavor. The joy of discovery, the aesthetic appreciation of an elegant proof, the creative leap that connects seemingly unrelated ideas — these are not things AI can replicate. The goal is to use AI to handle the routine, the tedious, and the large-scale, freeing human mathematicians to focus on what makes mathematics meaningful.

Can AI replace human mathematicians?#

No. Tao explicitly argues that AI should augment rather than replace human mathematicians. AI excels at handling the “long tail” of medium-difficulty problems and enabling large-scale systematic exploration, but the creative insight, aesthetic judgment, and conceptual leaps that drive mathematical progress remain uniquely human. Tao’s vision is for AI to “enlarge the pie, not compete with existing work.”

Citizen mathematics is the idea that non-professional mathematicians can contribute to mathematical research, analogous to citizen science in astronomy and biology. Enabled by formal verification tools that automatically check correctness, citizen mathematics lowers the barrier to entry so that skilled amateurs, programmers, and domain experts can participate. The Equational Theories Project, which included non-professional collaborators, is an early example of this model in action.
