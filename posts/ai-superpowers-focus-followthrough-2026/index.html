AI Superpowers Focus and Followthrough: Why Developers Must Master These Skills in 2026

AI makes developers 2-100x faster, yet burnout is rising. Focus and followthrough — not speed — are the real superpowers AI developers need in 2026.

AI can help developers complete tasks 2 to 100 times faster than before, yet burnout among developers is rising, not falling. The reason is a paradox at the heart of modern AI-assisted development: when you can do everything faster, you try to do everything — and end up finishing nothing. The real superpowers in the AI age are not speed or volume, but focus and followthrough. Developers who master these two skills will ship more, stress less, and build software that actually matters.

The Burnout Paradox — Why AI Speed Doesn’t Equal Less Stress#

The promise of AI coding tools was simple: remove friction, reduce toil, and let developers focus on creative work. In practice, something unexpected happened. A 2026 survey on Hacker News (story 49057897) revealed that developers using AI tools report feeling more pressure, not less. The always-on productivity expectation created by AI is a new and significant source of stress.

Why does this happen? When a task that used to take a day now takes an hour, the natural human response is not to take the remaining seven hours off. It is to start seven more tasks. AI removes the bottleneck of writing code, but it does not remove the cognitive load of managing, reviewing, debugging, and deploying that code. As developer Aniruddha noted in his analysis of the AI productivity trap, “Remove one bottleneck and the constraint moves to review, verification, deployment, and debugging.”

The result is a workforce that produces more tokens, more commits, and more proof-of-concept projects — but experiences higher mental fatigue, more context switching, and a growing sense of never being done. Developers report that AI tools create a “flow state” that is hard to step away from, paradoxically increasing mental exhaustion even as raw output rises.

The “ALL THE THINGS” Trap — How AI Encourages Project Proliferation#

Rick Manelius, author of the original essay “The New AI Superpowers: Focus and Followthrough,” describes a phenomenon he calls the “ALL THE THINGS” trap. When AI makes it trivially easy to scaffold a new project, generate boilerplate, and produce working prototypes, developers naturally start more projects. The cost of starting has dropped to near zero, so the rational response is to start everything.

The result is a developer who has 40 or more proof-of-concept projects running simultaneously. Each one is a few hours of AI-assisted work. None of them is finished. None of them is deployed. None of them is generating value.

This is the seductive lie of infinite productivity: that more output equals more impact. In reality, the developer with 40 half-finished projects has less impact than the developer who shipped one polished, production-quality application. The “vibe coding” phenomenon — where developers use AI to rapidly prototype ideas without deep understanding of the code being generated — has created a generation of developers who are excellent at starting and terrible at finishing.

The Hacker News community (story 49057877, 136 points) identified an organizational pattern that compounds this problem: teams using AI tools end up building incompatible versions of the same beginner-level software because AI makes it too easy to start from scratch rather than extend existing work. Every developer builds their own authentication system, their own API client, their own data layer — and none of them integrate.

Essentialism for Developers — Doing Less But Better in the AI Age#

Greg McKeown’s philosophy of Essentialism — “less but better” — has never been more relevant than in the age of AI-assisted development. The core insight is simple: instead of using AI to do more things, use AI to do the right things better.

For developers, this means a fundamental shift in strategy. The default mode with AI tools is horizontal expansion: start more projects, explore more ideas, generate more code. The essentialist mode is vertical deepening: take one project and push it further than you could without AI. Go deeper, not wider.

Consider two developers:

  • Developer A uses AI to start 10 side projects in a month. Each one gets a working prototype, a README, and a GitHub repo. None get users, feedback, or production deployment.
  • Developer B uses AI to ship one project to production, add real user authentication, write comprehensive tests, set up CI/CD, and iterate based on user feedback.

Developer B has created more value with less code. The AI superpower is not generating more — it is generating better by removing the friction that used to prevent developers from going the extra mile.

The practical application of essentialism in AI development is ruthless prioritization. Before you ask AI to generate code, ask yourself: “Is this the most important thing I could be working on right now?” If the answer is no, do not generate the code. The cost of generating code is near zero, but the cost of maintaining, reviewing, and integrating that code is not.

Partial vs Total Eclipse — Why the Last 1% Matters 100x More#

Garry Tan, venture capitalist and former Y Combinator partner, introduced a powerful analogy that Manelius applies directly to AI development: the difference between a partial solar eclipse and a total solar eclipse.

A partial eclipse — where the moon covers 99% of the sun — is a mildly interesting astronomical event. A total eclipse — where the moon covers 100% — is a life-changing experience that people travel across the world to witness. The difference between 99% and 100% is not 1%. It is the difference between “interesting” and “unforgettable.”

In software, the same principle applies. A project that is 99% complete — the core feature works, but there are no tests, no error handling, no documentation, no deployment pipeline — is a partial eclipse. It is interesting but not valuable. A project that is 100% complete — polished, tested, deployed, documented — is a total eclipse. It creates real value for users.

With AI, there is no excuse not to pay the price of that last 1%. The friction that used to make the final polish painful — writing documentation, adding error states, setting up monitoring — is now dramatically reduced by AI tools. Developers who use AI to push through the last mile, rather than to start the next project, are the ones who create total eclipses.

The AI Productivity Trap — More Tokens, Less Shipping#

Aniruddha’s analysis of the AI productivity trap, drawing on his experience at Mixpanel, identifies a critical insight: AI usage metrics (tokens consumed, prompts sent, code generated) are rising exponentially, but shipping rates are not keeping pace. Everyone is doing more, but the proportion of work that reaches production is shrinking.

The path of least resistance with AI is code generation. It feels productive. You see output immediately. You get a dopamine hit from watching code appear. But code generation without a clear goal, without an end state in mind, is busywork dressed up as productivity.

The solution, according to Aniruddha, is to treat AI as a non-deterministic blackbox pointed at a goal, not a task monkey. Instead of asking AI to “write a function that does X,” define the end state you want to reach and let the AI churn toward it. This shifts the developer’s role from code producer to goal definer and quality gate — a higher-value role that requires focus and followthrough, not just prompt engineering.

Chesterton’s Fence — Why Some Friction Is Good for Developers#

G.K. Chesterton’s famous principle states: “Do not remove a fence until you understand why it was put there.” Applied to AI and developer productivity, this principle reveals a uncomfortable truth: some of the friction that AI removes served a purpose.

The friction of writing code by hand forced developers to think before they typed. The friction of setting up a project manually forced developers to understand the architecture. The friction of debugging forced developers to trace through logic and build mental models of their systems. When AI removes all this friction, it also removes the learning and understanding that came with it.

A developer who uses AI to generate a complex function without understanding it has not gained a superpower. They have gained a dependency. When that function breaks in production — and it will — they lack the mental model to debug it.

The Hacker News discussion on “The Cost of Seamlessness” (story 49048352) made this point powerfully: people do not need 100% ease. They need challenges they can manage at a motivating rate. A frictionless development experience may not be desirable because some resistance builds skill, understanding, and satisfaction.

The practical takeaway is not to avoid AI tools, but to use them deliberately. Use AI to remove bad friction — boilerplate, repetitive tasks, rote work — but keep good friction — architectural decisions, code review, debugging, understanding. The developer who can distinguish between the two will outperform the developer who removes all friction indiscriminately.

Practical Strategies for Focus and Followthrough with AI#

The theory is clear, but how do developers actually implement focus and followthrough in their daily work with AI tools? Here are actionable strategies drawn from the research and community discussion.

1. Define the end state before generating code. Before you open your AI coding tool, write down what “done” looks like. What is the user experience? What are the acceptance criteria? What does success look like? This single practice transforms AI from a task monkey into a goal-oriented tool.

2. Limit your active projects to three. The research is clear: developers with 40+ active projects finish none of them. Pick three — one primary, one secondary, one experimental — and do not start a fourth until one of the three is shipped or killed. Use AI to finish the primary project faster, not to start the fourth.

3. Use AI for the last mile, not just the first mile. Most developers use AI to scaffold projects and write initial code. The real leverage is using AI to finish: write tests, add error handling, generate documentation, set up deployment. The last 1% is where the total eclipse happens.

4. Review every line of AI-generated code. This is non-negotiable. AI-generated code is probabilistic, not deterministic. It can contain subtle bugs, security vulnerabilities, and architectural mistakes that a human reviewer must catch. Treat AI output as a first draft, not a final product.

5. Schedule deliberate non-coding time. The always-on productivity expectation created by AI is a real threat to developer wellbeing. Schedule time for thinking, reading, reviewing, and resting. The best code is often written after a walk, not after a fourth consecutive hour of prompting.

6. Apply Chesterton’s fence to your workflow. Before you automate or delegate a task to AI, ask: “Why was this task done manually? What purpose does the friction serve?” If the friction was teaching you something or enforcing quality, keep it. If it was just tedious, remove it.

7. Track shipping, not tokens. Measure what matters. Do not track how many prompts you sent or how many lines of code AI generated. Track how many features shipped, how many bugs were fixed, how many users were served. Shipping is the only metric that counts.

Conclusion — The New Superpowers Are Focus and Followthrough#

The AI revolution in software development has delivered on its promise of speed. Developers can now generate code, scaffold projects, and prototype ideas faster than ever before. But speed without direction is just noise. The developers who will thrive in the AI age are not the ones who generate the most code. They are the ones who choose what to build, finish what they start, and push through the last 1% that transforms a partial eclipse into a total one.

Focus and followthrough are the new AI superpowers. Speed is the commodity. Every developer has access to the same AI tools. The differentiator is the discipline to use those tools with intention — to go vertical instead of horizontal, to finish instead of start, to ship instead of prototype.

The developer who masters focus and followthrough will not just write more code. They will write code that matters. They will ship products that users love. They will build careers that last. And they will do it without burning out.

The tools are in your hands. The question is: will you use them to do everything, or to do what matters?

Frequently Asked Questions#

Q: What is the AI productivity paradox for developers? A: The AI productivity paradox is the observation that AI tools make developers 2-100x faster at individual tasks, yet developer burnout is rising. This happens because developers use their newfound speed to start more projects rather than finish existing ones, leading to cognitive overload, context switching, and a growing sense of never being done.

Q: How can developers avoid the “ALL THE THINGS” trap with AI? A: Developers can avoid this trap by limiting active projects to three, defining the end state before generating code, and using AI to finish projects (testing, documentation, deployment) rather than just to start them. The key is shifting from horizontal expansion (more projects) to vertical deepening (better projects).

Q: What is the partial vs total eclipse analogy in AI development? A: Garry Tan’s analogy compares a 99% complete project to a partial solar eclipse (mildly interesting) and a 100% complete project to a total solar eclipse (life-changing). The last 1% of effort — polish, testing, documentation, deployment — creates a 100x difference in user impact. AI makes achieving that last 1% easier than ever.

Q: Does AI increase or decrease developer burnout? A: Current evidence suggests AI increases burnout for many developers. While AI removes coding friction, it creates an always-on productivity expectation, encourages excessive context switching, and generates a “flow state” that is hard to step away from. Developers who use AI with intentional focus and boundaries report better outcomes than those who use it indiscriminately.

Q: What is Chesterton’s fence and how does it apply to AI coding tools? A: Chesterton’s fence is the principle that you should not remove something until you understand why it was put there. Applied to AI, it means some development friction — like writing code by hand, debugging, and architectural thinking — serves a purpose in building understanding and skill. Developers should use AI to remove bad friction (tedious boilerplate) while preserving good friction (learning, quality enforcement, deliberate design).
