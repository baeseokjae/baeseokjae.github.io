Introduction — Kimi K3 Arrives as the First Open 3T-Class Model#

On July 16, 2026, Moonshot AI released Kimi K3, a 2.8 trillion parameter mixture-of-experts model that claims the title of the first openly available 3T-class model. With a 1 million token context window, architectural innovations in attention mechanisms, and a promised open-weight release by July 27, K3 immediately became one of the most discussed AI releases of the year. But for many developers and researchers, the first real interaction with K3 came not through a benchmark leaderboard, but through a simple, almost whimsical test: asking it to draw a pelican in SVG. Simon Willison’s Pelican benchmark, originally a casual experiment, has become a de facto “hello world” for new models. This article examines what Kimi K3’s performance on the Pelican benchmark reveals about the model’s reasoning behavior, cost structure, and hidden design choices — and what the test still teaches us about frontier AI in 2026.

What Is the Pelican Benchmark and Why Does It Still Matter?#

The Pelican benchmark is not a formal evaluation suite. It is a single prompt: “Generate an SVG of a pelican standing on a beach.” Simon Willison, a well-known Python developer and AI commentator, created it as a quick, informal way to test a model’s ability to follow instructions, generate valid code, and produce visually coherent output. The test is cheap, fast, and surprisingly revealing.

A Forcing Function for Hands-On Testing#

In an era dominated by massive leaderboards like Artificial Analysis, Chatbot Arena, and SWE-bench, the Pelican benchmark serves a different purpose. It is a forcing function — a low-friction way to get hands-on with a new model within minutes of its API going live. You do not need a curated evaluation harness, a GPU cluster, or a standardized dataset. You need a single prompt and a few cents of API credit.

Simon Willison himself has acknowledged that the Pelican benchmark’s correlation to overall model quality is “mostly severed now.” High-end models all produce reasonable pelican SVGs. But the test remains useful for several specific purposes:

  • Cost and reasoning estimation: How many tokens does the model spend on a simple task?
  • SVG validity check: Does the output render correctly, or are there syntax errors?
  • Model family comparison: How do different versions of the same model family evolve?
  • Behavioral fingerprinting: What hidden instructions or system prompts influence the output?

The Pelican as a Diagnostic Tool#

What makes the Pelican benchmark valuable in 2026 is not its ability to rank models, but its ability to reveal model behavior. When a model spends 13,241 reasoning tokens on a simple SVG, that tells you something about its default reasoning effort. When a hidden system prompt inflates token counts, that tells you something about the provider’s design philosophy. The Pelican benchmark has evolved from an intelligence proxy into a diagnostic tool — and that shift is itself instructive.

Kimi K3’s Pelican — A $0.25 Hello World#

Simon Willison ran the Pelican benchmark on Kimi K3 shortly after the API launched. The results were revealing. K3 generated a pelican SVG at a cost of approximately $0.25, consuming 13,241 reasoning tokens out of 16,658 total output tokens. That means roughly 80% of the output was reasoning tokens — the model’s internal chain-of-thought before producing the final SVG.

This is not necessarily a sign of inefficiency. It reflects a design choice: K3 is built to reason deeply by default, treating even simple tasks as opportunities for extended deliberation.

Is Max-Effort Always Necessary?#

The question this raises is whether a simple SVG generation requires 13,241 reasoning tokens. For a task that could be solved with a few hundred tokens of planning and a straightforward SVG template, K3’s approach feels like using a sledgehammer to crack a nut. Moonshot has announced that low and high reasoning effort modes are coming, which should bring costs down significantly for simple tasks. Until then, every Pelican SVG from K3 costs a quarter — a steep price for a “hello world.”

The Hidden System Prompt Mystery#

One of the most intriguing findings from Simon Willison’s testing was evidence of a hidden system prompt in Kimi K3. By measuring token counts for simple inputs, Willison detected that the word “hi” consumed 86 tokens instead of the expected ~10. This discrepancy suggests an injected system prompt of approximately 85 tokens that is prepended to every conversation.

What Is Moonshot Injecting?#

The exact content of the hidden system prompt is unknown, but its presence raises important questions. Hidden system prompts are not unique to Moonshot — many providers inject instructions for safety, formatting, or behavior steering. However, the practice is controversial because it affects token counts, costs, and the transparency of the model’s behavior.

Why Hidden Prompts Matter for Users#

For developers building on top of K3, the hidden system prompt has practical implications. It inflates input token counts, increasing costs for every API call. It may also influence the model’s behavior in ways that are not documented. If the hidden prompt includes instructions about safety, formatting, or refusal patterns, users need to know about it to understand and predict model behavior. The lack of transparency around these injected prompts is a growing concern in the AI community, and K3’s case highlights the need for clearer disclosure.

Beyond the Pelican benchmark, Kimi K3 delivers strong results across established evaluation suites. On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, K3 ranks #4 out of 187 models with a score of 57, placing it just behind the frontier leaders.

Frontend Code Arena #1#

One area where K3 excels is frontend code generation. It ranks #1 on the Frontend Code Arena, surpassing even Claude Fable 5. This is a significant achievement given that Fable 5 is widely regarded as one of the strongest coding models available. K3’s strength in frontend tasks aligns with Moonshot’s focus on practical, user-facing applications.

The Verbosity Problem#

K3’s performance comes with a cost — literally and figuratively. On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, K3 consumes 130 million output tokens versus the average of 63 million. That is more than double the average token consumption. This verbosity is a direct consequence of the max reasoning effort mode. While it may produce better results on complex tasks, it makes K3 significantly more expensive to run at scale.

Pricing and Practicality — Most Expensive Chinese Model Yet#

Kimi K3 is priced at $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output tokens. This makes it the most expensive Chinese AI lab model to date, matching the pricing tier of Claude Sonnet.

The Cache Discount#

One notable feature is K3’s cache hit pricing: $0.30 per million input tokens, a 90% discount. Moonshot’s Mooncake disaggregated inference architecture achieves a 90%+ cache hit rate in coding workloads, which means frequent users can dramatically reduce their effective costs. For high-volume applications with repetitive prompt patterns, the cache discount makes K3 significantly more affordable than the list price suggests.

Signaling Confidence or Cost Pressure?#

K3’s pricing strategy is noteworthy. Chinese AI labs have historically competed on price, undercutting Western models to gain market share. K3 breaks this pattern. By pricing at parity with Sonnet — a premium tier — Moonshot is signaling confidence in K3’s quality. The message is clear: K3 is not a budget alternative; it is a frontier competitor. Whether the market accepts this positioning remains to be seen, especially given the availability of cheaper alternatives like DeepSeek V4 at $0.50/M input.

Architectural Innovations — KDA, AttnRes, and 16/896 MoE#

Kimi K3 is built on two key architectural innovations: Kimi Delta Attention (KDA) and Attention Residuals (AttnRes). These are not marketing buzzwords — they represent genuine advances in how the model processes information.

Kimi Delta Attention (KDA)#

KDA is a novel attention mechanism designed to improve the efficiency of long-context processing. With a 1 million token context window, traditional attention mechanisms would be prohibitively expensive. KDA reduces the computational cost of attention by focusing on the most relevant parts of the context, enabling K3 to handle book-length inputs without proportional increases in compute.

Attention Residuals (AttnRes)#

AttnRes introduces residual connections within the attention mechanism itself. This allows information to flow more effectively through the model’s layers, improving gradient propagation during training and enabling the model to learn more complex patterns. The combination of KDA and AttnRes is what makes K3’s 2.8 trillion parameter count feasible without requiring impractical amounts of compute.

Mixture of Experts at Scale#

K3 uses a Mixture of Experts architecture with 896 total experts, of which 16 are activated per token. This means that despite having 2.8 trillion total parameters, only a fraction are used for any given forward pass. The effective compute per token is comparable to a much smaller dense model, while the total parameter count provides the capacity for a vast amount of learned knowledge.

The Pelican’s Limitations — What It Doesn’t Test#

For all its utility as a quick diagnostic, the Pelican benchmark has significant blind spots. Understanding these limitations is essential for interpreting what K3’s Pelican performance does and does not tell us.

No Agentic Tool Calling#

The Pelican benchmark tests a single, self-contained generation task. It does not test a model’s ability to use tools, call APIs, browse the web, or interact with external systems. In 2026, agentic capabilities — the ability to plan, execute multi-step tasks, and recover from errors — are arguably more important than raw generation quality. K3’s DeepSWE score of 67.3 on the mini-SWE-agent harness provides some signal about its agentic capabilities, but the Pelican benchmark offers none.

No Long-Context Reliability#

The Pelican benchmark involves a single prompt and a single response. It does not test how a model handles long conversations, retrieves information from extended contexts, or maintains coherence over thousands of tokens. Given K3’s 1 million token context window, this is a significant gap. A model that generates beautiful SVGs but cannot reliably retrieve information from the middle of a long document is not truly useful for knowledge work.

No Multi-Turn Consistency#

The Pelican benchmark is a one-shot test. It does not evaluate a model’s ability to follow instructions across multiple turns, incorporate feedback, or refine its output based on user requests. For real-world applications, multi-turn consistency is critical.

What We Can Still Learn from the Pelican in 2026#

Despite its limitations, the Pelican benchmark remains valuable in 2026 for several specific purposes.

A Cost and Efficiency Baseline#

The Pelican benchmark provides a standardized, repeatable task for measuring a model’s cost efficiency. When K3 spends $0.25 and 13,241 reasoning tokens on a simple SVG, that is a data point. When a future model does the same task for $0.02 and 500 reasoning tokens, that is also a data point. The Pelican benchmark gives us a common reference point for comparing efficiency across models and over time.

A Behavioral Fingerprint#

The Pelican benchmark reveals model behavior that formal benchmarks miss. The hidden system prompt detection, the reasoning token explosion, the verbosity patterns — these are behavioral fingerprints that tell us how a model is designed and what assumptions its creators made. In an industry where model cards are often incomplete and system prompts are hidden, the Pelican benchmark serves as a lightweight auditing tool.

A Community Ritual#

There is also an intangible value to the Pelican benchmark. It is a shared experience — a ritual that connects the AI community. When a new model launches, the first question is often “Does it pass the Pelican test?” This shared reference point creates a common language for discussing model quality, even if the correlation to real-world performance is imperfect.

The Future of Informal Evals#

The Pelican benchmark’s evolution from an accidental intelligence proxy to a known, gameable metric mirrors the lifecycle of all benchmarks. As Simon Willison noted, the correlation to model quality is “mostly severed now.” But that does not mean the benchmark is useless. It means its purpose has shifted. The Pelican benchmark is no longer a test of intelligence. It is a test of behavior, efficiency, and transparency. And those are tests worth running.

Conclusion — K3’s Place in the Frontier Landscape#

Kimi K3 is a remarkable achievement. It is the first openly available 3T-class model, built on genuine architectural innovations, with competitive benchmark scores and a promised open-weight release. Its performance on the Pelican benchmark reveals both its strengths and its growing pains: deep reasoning capabilities undermined by a one-size-fits-all max effort mode, frontier-level quality at a premium price, and a hidden system prompt that raises transparency concerns.

The Pelican benchmark, for all its simplicity, continues to serve a valuable purpose. It gives us a quick, cheap, and revealing first look at new models. It tells us about costs, reasoning patterns, and design philosophy. It is not a substitute for rigorous evaluation, but it is an excellent complement to it.

As K3 prepares for its open-weight release on July 27, the AI community will have an opportunity to examine the model directly, fine-tune it, and integrate it into local workflows. The Pelican benchmark will be there to greet it — a simple test that still has something to teach us.
