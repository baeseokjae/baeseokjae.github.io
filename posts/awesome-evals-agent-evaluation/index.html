Awesome Evals (benchflow-ai/awesome-evals) is a curated, opinionated library of 443+ annotated resources for building and evaluating AI agents, assembled through a depth-4 recursive citation crawl of 11,600 papers, 47 transcribed talks, and per-section gap audits. Unlike most “awesome” lists that are simple link dumps, every entry in Awesome Evals includes a clear explanation of what it is and why it belongs, with verified URLs, verbatim quotes, and dead links pruned. The repository has earned 744 GitHub stars and 65 forks since its launch in June 2026, and it comes with a PATTERNS.md playbook containing runnable code for the most important evaluation techniques.

What Is Awesome Evals? The “Non-BS” Curated Library for AI Agent Evaluation#

Awesome Evals is maintained by BenchFlow, an AI evaluation infrastructure company, and lives at github.com/benchflow-ai/awesome-evals. It was created on June 24, 2026, and has quickly become the definitive starting point for anyone building or evaluating AI agents.

The library was assembled using four complementary methods:

  1. Depth-4 recursive citation crawl — 11,600 papers ranked by in-degree to surface the academic canon
  2. Targeted practitioner-web discovery — industry sources that citation graphs miss, including Eugene Yan, Han-Chung Lee, Hamel Husain, Shreya Shankar, and Nathan Lambert
  3. 47 talks and podcasts transcribed and deep-noted — with verbatim quotes and timestamps
  4. Per-section gap audits — with adversarial verification to ensure nothing important is missing

The result is 443+ curated links and 146 deep reading notes organized into 10 core sections, plus a must-read starter set of 12 canonical sources.

Why Agent Evaluation Matters More Than Ever#

The AI agent market reached $11.8 billion in 2026, with 93% of enterprises expressing intent to deploy AI agents. Yet only 23% have reached production scale — a staggering 70-point deployment gap (Axis Intelligence / Gartner, McKinsey, Deloitte). The global AI agents market is forecast to hit $50.31 billion by 2030 at a 45.8% CAGR (Grand View Research, 2025), and 40% of enterprise applications are adding AI agents in 2026 with a 79% adoption rate (SQ Magazine).

Agent evaluation is the bottleneck to production deployment. The shift from chat-based LLM applications to autonomous agents introduces fundamentally harder evaluation challenges. As Han-Chung Lee put it: “Chat eval was a spreadsheet; agent eval is a system.”

The Anatomy of Awesome Evals: 443+ Curated Links, 146 Deep Notes, 47 Transcribed Talks#

What sets Awesome Evals apart from other awesome lists is the depth of curation. Each entry is annotated with a clear explanation of its contribution, and the deep reading notes in the notes/ directory contain verbatim quotes with timestamps from talks and podcasts.

The citation crawl methodology is particularly noteworthy. By running a depth-4 recursive crawl of 11,600 papers ranked by in-degree, the maintainers surfaced the true academic canon rather than relying on popularity or recency bias. This was combined with practitioner-web discovery to catch the industry sources that citation graphs systematically miss — blog posts, newsletters, and conference talks that are often more practical than academic papers.

The 47 transcribed talks and podcasts include major voices in the field: Hamel Husain, Shreya Shankar, Eugene Yan, Ankur Goyal, Jason Wei, Dawn Song, Nathan Lambert, Andrej Karpathy, and many more. Each transcription includes timestamps and key quotes, making it easy to find specific insights without watching hours of video.

The Must-Read Starter Set: 12 Canonical Sources#

Awesome Evals includes a curated starter set of 12 sources that every practitioner should read first. These form the foundation of agent evaluation knowledge:

  1. “The Second Half” — Shunyu Yao — The field-level argument that evaluation becomes more important than training
  2. “An LLM-as-Judge Won’t Save the Product, Fixing Your Process Will” — Eugene Yan — Process over tooling; evals as the scientific method
  3. “Hidden Technical Debt: Agent Evaluation Infrastructure” — Han-Chung Lee — Control/data plane, the five eval surfaces, state deltas
  4. “LLM Evals FAQ” — Hamel Husain & Shreya Shankar — The densest operational Q&A on error analysis and binary judgments
  5. “Asymmetry of Verification and Verifier’s Law” — Jason Wei — Ability to verify equals ability to create an RL environment
  6. “Demystifying Evals for AI Agents” — Anthropic — Best primary source on agent-specific evals: task design, outcome vs trajectory, pass@k vs pass^k
  7. “How to Build Good Language Modeling Benchmarks” — Ofir Press — Natural, auto-evaluatable, challenging benchmarks
  8. “AI Agents That Matter” — Kapoor et al. — Cost as a first-class metric; model-dev vs app-dev

  10. “Who Validates the Validators? (EvalGen)” — Shankar et al. — Criteria drift: you can’t write the rubric before you grade
  11. “Benches 2026” — Florian Brand — Why benchmarks break in the agent era
  12. “A Shared Playbook for Trustworthy Third-Party Evaluations” — OpenAI — Standards for independent frontier-model evaluations

The 10 Core Sections of the Library#

Awesome Evals is organized into 10 sections that cover the full spectrum of agent evaluation:

Section 1: Why We Need Evals#

This section makes the case that evaluation is the most important bottleneck in AI development. Key sources include Shunyu Yao’s “The Second Half” (evaluation becomes more important than training), Eugene Yan’s eval-as-scientific-method framework, and Hamel Husain’s argument that “error analysis is consistently the highest-ROI activity.”

Section 2: Eval ⇄ Capability ⇄ RL Environment#

Jason Wei’s Verifier’s Law states that trainability tracks verifiability — if you can evaluate something, you can build an RL environment for it. This section includes Han-Chung Lee’s taxonomy of RL environments, the DeepSeek-R1 paper (pure RL with verifiable rewards makes reasoning emerge), and Prime Intellect’s verifiers package that makes the eval-is-an-RL-env thesis concrete.

Section 3: The Model / Harness / Skill Decomposition#

Understanding the separation between the model being evaluated, the harness that runs the evaluation, and the skills being measured is critical for building reliable evaluations.

Section 4: Observability and the Output/Eval Space#

The surfaces you can grade — output text, tool calls, intermediate reasoning, world state changes, and final outcomes. This section covers tracing, logging, and the five eval surfaces identified by Han-Chung Lee.

Section 5: Evaluation Infrastructure#

The full eval stack: datasets, scorers, online/offline evaluation, tracing, and CI integration. This is where the “hidden technical debt” of agent evaluation lives.

Section 6: Benchmark vs. Eval#

A critical distinction often blurred in practice. Benchmarks are standardized, frozen tests for comparison; evals are contextual, evolving measurements for improvement. This section covers contamination, saturation, label errors, and leaderboard gaming.

Section 7: Evals and RL Environments#

Verifiers, reward design, difficulty calibration, and the lifecycle of RL environments. The Goldilocks band for difficulty (1–4/16) and the economics of environment quality.

Section 8: LLM-as-Judge and Verifiers#

Alignment, biases, verifiable vs. judgeable tasks. The key insight: “verifiable beats judgeable” — whenever you can write a programmatic check, do that instead of using an LLM judge.

Section 9: Agent-Specific Evaluation#

Trajectories, tool use, multi-turn interactions, world state verification, multi-agent scenarios, and localization. This is the hardest and fastest-evolving area.

Section 10: Safety and Adversarial Evaluation#

Prompt injection, jailbreaks, action-authorization, benchmark auditing, and the OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications. Includes BenchJack’s 8-pattern flaw taxonomy and AgentDojo for prompt injection benchmarking.

The PATTERNS.md Playbook: Runnable Code for Real Evaluation#

One of the most valuable components of Awesome Evals is the PATTERNS.md playbook. Unlike most resources that describe evaluation techniques in the abstract, this playbook provides real, runnable code with worked examples for:

  • LLM-as-judge — aligned to human judgments with calibration techniques
  • Pass@k and pass^k — the distinction between sampling multiple times and succeeding on any (pass@k) versus succeeding on all (pass^k)
  • Error analysis — systematic categorization of failure modes
  • Trajectory and world-state grading — evaluating the full agent journey, not just the final output
  • CI gating — integrating evaluations into continuous integration pipelines
  • Verifiable rewards — programmatic checks that can be used for RL training

This playbook bridges the gap between theory and practice, making it possible for teams to implement production-grade evaluation without starting from scratch.

The Future of Agent Evaluation#

Several trends are shaping the future of agent evaluation, all well-documented in Awesome Evals:

RL environments as the new evaluation standard. The thesis that “if you can eval it, you can build an RL environment for it” is becoming mainstream. Prime Intellect’s Environments Hub already hosts 2,500+ shared eval/RL environments, and 35+ companies now sell RL environments (SemiAnalysis).

Verifiable rewards over LLM judges. The industry is shifting toward programmatic verification wherever possible. DeepSeek-R1 proved that pure RL with rule-based verifiable rewards (no SFT) can make reasoning emerge. Tülu 3 from AI2 popularized RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards) and open-sourced the recipe.

Contamination-resistant design. As models get better at recognizing when they’re being tested, evaluation design must evolve. OpenAI’s Deployment Simulation work showed that replaying ~1.3 million real conversations surfaced reward-hacking that no fixed eval caught.

The eval-driven development (EDD) methodology. Inspired by test-driven development, EDD treats evals as the scientific method: observe data → annotate → hypothesize → experiment → measure. The 50:50 pass/fail split for balanced datasets and the cycle of continuous improvement are becoming standard practice.

Frequently Asked Questions#

What makes Awesome Evals different from other awesome lists?#

Most awesome lists are link dumps with minimal curation. Awesome Evals is annotated and verified: every entry says what it is and why it belongs, URLs are checked, quotes are verbatim, and dead or abandoned tools are pruned rather than silently listed. It was assembled through a depth-4 citation crawl of 11,600 papers, practitioner-web discovery, and 47 transcribed talks.

How many resources does Awesome Evals contain?#

The library contains 443+ curated links and 146 deep reading notes, organized into 10 core sections plus a must-read starter set of 12 canonical sources. It also includes a PATTERNS.md playbook with runnable code for key evaluation techniques.

Who maintains Awesome Evals?#

Awesome Evals is maintained by BenchFlow, an AI evaluation infrastructure company. The repository is open-source and accepts contributions from the community via its CONTRIBUTING.md guidelines.

Is Awesome Evals useful for beginners or only for experts?#

Both. The must-read starter set of 12 sources is designed for newcomers, while the 10 core sections and 146 deep reading notes provide depth for experienced practitioners. The PATTERNS.md playbook bridges theory and practice with runnable code examples.

How is agent evaluation different from chat evaluation?#

Agent evaluation is fundamentally harder than chat evaluation due to multi-turn interactions, tool use, state modification, and complex error recovery. As Han-Chung Lee noted, “Chat eval was a spreadsheet; agent eval is a system.” Agent evaluation requires tracking trajectories, verifying world state changes, and grading tool selection accuracy — none of which apply to simple chat applications.
