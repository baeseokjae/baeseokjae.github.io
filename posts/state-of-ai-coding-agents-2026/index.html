State of AI Coding Agents 2026: What Peer-Reviewed and Field Studies Actually Show

Dated evidence tables separating controlled studies from vendor claims about AI coding agents in 2026, including what the research still cannot show.

The state of AI coding agents in 2026 is not one number, and the studies that actually control for something disagree on direction. A randomised trial on experienced open-source maintainers found that allowing early-2025 frontier AI tools made their own tasks 19% slower, while a Google enterprise trial and a 2022 GitHub Copilot experiment both found speed-ups — roughly 21% and 55.8%. Those results only look contradictory if you skip the populations, the task types and the tool generations behind them. Read together, they say something more useful and less quotable: the measured effect of AI-assisted coding depends heavily on who is coding, on what, with which generation of tool, and on how long the observation window is (METR, arXiv:2507.09089).

This page is a dated refresh of an earlier market narrative. The previous version opened with session metrics (23-minute sessions, 47 tool calls per session) and market sizing figures ($12.8 billion, 84% adoption, 51% daily use) presented as established fact. A bounded retrieval sweep on 2026-10-23 could not trace any of those specific figures to a primary source, so they are withdrawn from the body rather than relabelled as vendor data I had not read. What replaces them is what a reader arriving with research intent was actually looking for: a table of controlled studies with venue, sample size, design and effect direction, and a separate record of what this page no longer claims.

Three points about that table matter more than any single row. First, exactly one of the four rows is peer-reviewed; the other three are arXiv working papers, and two of those three have authors affiliated with the companies whose tools they study. Second, no row measures a multi-week agentic workflow — every one of them measures time-on-task or task security inside a bounded study session. Third, none of the numbers can be averaged: the populations are disjoint, the tasks are different in kind, and the tool generations span 2022 to 2025.

Why the three percentages do not cancel out#

The temptation is to average 55.8%, 21% and −19% into a single “AI coding agents make developers X% faster” claim. That would be a scope error, not a synthesis. The Copilot experiment measured 95 freelancers building one well-specified synthetic server with a 2022 autocomplete-era tool (arXiv:2302.06590v1). The Google trial measured 96 engineers on a single complex enterprise task with company-internal features (arXiv:2410.12944v3). The METR trial measured 16 maintainers working on their own mature repositories where they already held roughly five years of context — precisely the situation in which an AI agent’s suggestions compete with knowledge the developer already has (arXiv:2507.09089v2).

The METR result is worth reading closely for a second reason: the gap between belief and measurement. The same developers predicted a 24% speed-up before the tasks and estimated a 20% speed-up afterwards, while the instrumented measurement showed a 19% slowdown. Any survey that asks developers how much faster AI makes them is measuring that estimate, not the instrumented outcome. The productivity-metric argument itself is covered in more depth in the AI coding productivity paradox analysis, which is the right place to continue that thread rather than re-arguing it here.

The peer-reviewed record is thinner than the market narrative implies#

The research-intent question behind this refresh was whether there is peer-reviewed evidence about human-AI pair programming and about the safety difference between assistants and autonomous agents. There is some, and it is narrower than the volume of 2026 commentary suggests. Security outcomes do have a peer-reviewed controlled study: the CCS ‘23 experiment found both worse code security and higher confidence among participants with assistant access (arXiv:2211.03622v3).

Peer-reviewed HCI venues also publish controlled user studies of proactive AI programming support — for example Assistance or Disruption? Exploring and Evaluating the Design and Trade-offs of Proactive AI Programming Support at CHI ‘25, which reports a within-subject code-editor experiment across three versions of the Codellaborator system with 18 participants (ACM DL, DOI 10.1145/3706598.3713357). It is cited here at venue, design and sample-size level only: its effect sizes and interaction measures were not extracted during this refresh, so no outcome figure from that study appears on this page. A future revision that reads the full text can promote it to an effect-bearing row; until then this page records only what its abstract states — proactive suggestions increased efficiency against the prompt-only baseline while also disrupting workflow — and no effect size.

Autonomy versus assistant: what “safer” can and cannot mean yet#

Asked directly, is a fully autonomous coding agent safer or less safe than a pair-programming assistant? The honest answer as of this refresh is that no controlled study answers that comparison head-to-head. There is no trial that randomises the same tasks across an autonomous-agent condition and an assistant condition and measures security, review burden and defect escape rate. Anyone presenting a confident ranking is extrapolating.

What the evidence does support is narrower and still decision-relevant. In the assistant era, having AI write code did not make participants more careful — it made them more confident while producing less secure code (arXiv:2211.03622v3). That finding is about self-assessment as much as about generated code, and it transfers to autonomy as a warning about review design rather than as a measured effect of autonomy. The practical consequence is that autonomy should be treated as a risk-control decision — reversibility, CI gates, human review on security-sensitive paths — rather than as a safety label that research has validated. The controlled comparison that would settle the question does not exist yet.

Where the evidence is thin, and how this was checked#

The evidence base behind this page was assembled as a bounded retrieval sweep, not an experiment: five candidate sources were queried by known identifier through the arXiv Atom API and an ACM Digital Library landing page, all five were retrieved, and raw payloads with SHA-256 hashes were kept as a private author verification log. That log is not published as a downloadable dataset, because the payloads are third-party HTML; the claims above rest on the abstracts and landing pages cited inline, which any reader can open at the URLs given.

The sweep has limits worth stating plainly. It was not an exhaustive crawl of CHI, ICSE or FSE proceedings, so the absence of other studies is not established — only that these five were verified within the time budget. Only two of the five sources are peer-reviewed, and just one of the four table rows is. For the CHI ‘25 study only its venue, design and sample size were read, not its outcome measures. No quality, maintainability or review-burden outcome was found in any controlled design, which means the entire “does the code get better” question is currently answered by vendor commentary rather than by measurement.

What would change this picture#

Three kinds of evidence would move this page from a caution to a conclusion. A preregistered multi-week field trial that measures defect escape rate and review burden alongside time, rather than time alone. A head-to-head randomised comparison of an autonomous workflow against an assistant workflow on security-sensitive tasks, so that “safer” becomes a measurable direction instead of a design preference. And independent replication of the METR slowdown on a larger, non-volunteer population of maintainers — the single result most often quoted out of context on this topic. Until at least one of those exists, the defensible position is that effect direction is population-dependent and that any single headline percentage is a scope error.
